If your AI guru tells you Claude is the best agent, run.

Not because Claude is bad. Claude can be very good. Sometimes, excellent.

## The problem with "the best"

The problem is the phrase “the best.”

In AI, calling one agent the best without talking about limits, cost, context, pricing, and timing is not really analysis. It is a conclusion sold without the reasoning that should come with it.

> The tool that feels generous today may feel stingy tomorrow.

Digital services live inside an awkward tension: acquire users, monetize what they can, and keep costs under control.

Social platforms made that bargain familiar. The product looked free, but users paid with attention, data, and dependence. First the platform became useful. Then necessary. Then more restrictive.

## Why AI products move in cycles

LLMs work through a similar logic, with one harsher difference: heavy users are expensive.

Every long session, every coding agent, every oversized context window, every extended reasoning run burns real capacity. GPUs, power, infrastructure, queues, subsidies, and investor patience all sit behind the magic trick.

That is why these companies move in cycles.

First, they give you more than seems reasonable: better models, larger contexts, more messages, more headroom. The product feels magical.

Then come the adjustments: tighter limits, usage windows, less consistent models, more granular pricing, credits, plan tiers, and behavior changes nobody asked for.

I think of them as enshittification cycles.

## Why vendor loyalty is fragile

Claude, especially Claude Code, has recently been in an uncomfortable phase for heavy users: touchier limits, behavior changes, and quality issues that Anthropic itself eventually acknowledged in public.

That does not make Claude trash.

But it should be enough to make you suspicious of anyone selling it as “the best agent” without caveats.

On the other side, OpenAI currently feels more generous to many intensive users. ChatGPT feels roomy, Codex is pushing hard, and the relationship between capability, limits, and effective cost has become difficult to ignore.

But OpenAI is not outside the cycle.

If it captures enough heavy usage, it will also have to discipline the economics of that usage: credits, limits, consumption pricing, more expensive plans, or narrower windows.

And if Anthropic feels enough pressure, it has every reason to move the other way: increase capacity, relax limits, tune the product, and try to win users back.

## Build portable workflows instead

That is the point.

The tool that feels generous today may feel stingy tomorrow. The one that feels constrained now may come roaring back. The one that wins benchmarks this week may still lose in day-to-day work.

That is why marrying a tool is a bad strategy.

You should not be a fan of Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Cursor, Windsurf, or whichever logo people are defending this week.

You should have portable workflows.

> The skill is being able to change horses without your whole system falling apart.

Your prompts, repositories, skills, documents, criteria, and processes should not depend on your provider's business model.

This is not about picking a horse and arguing for it online. The skill is being able to change horses without your whole system falling apart.

In AI, the answer to “which model should I use?” changes constantly.

And if your system cannot change with it, the real problem is not Claude or OpenAI.

It is that you built your productivity on top of a platform you do not control.
